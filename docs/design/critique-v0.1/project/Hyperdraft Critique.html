Hyperdraft / Design Critique Prepared for Discordwell · 20.V.2026 · 14 spreads

Eight engines,
one frame — and the frame is the part you haven't designed yet.

A critique of Hyperdraft's current shape and a sketch toward a polyglot card-lab identity that makes the meta-game — the part where you switch between eight rules engines — the actual product.
01First impression

The tagline is doing all the work. Nothing else is.

"Eight engines, one frame" is the strongest line in the entire product surface, and right now it lives in a <title> tag. That is the brand. That is the pitch. That is the design system, in five words. It should be the first thing a visitor sees, set huge, and the rest of the page should answer the implied question: "…so what does the frame look like?"

Today, the answer is mostly "a React dev's default stylesheet." The repo is 96% Python, 3.4% TypeScript, 0.1% CSS. The split is honest — this is a research artifact, an event-pipeline pun made visible — and the UI reads like one. That can be a strength (think Are.na, Bear, Linear's early shell) but only if you commit to the genre. Right now it reads as unfinished rather than severe, which is the worst place to be.

The other signal that lands is the AI angle — "AI-powered deckbuilder that creates unique card game metas with dynamically generated rules." That's a different product from "rules engine for four canonical TCGs," and the README is conflicted about which one Hyperdraft is. The site has to pick. My recommendation, repeated below: pick the lab, not the playground.

The cleanest read of Hyperdraft is not "play MTG online." It's "a workbench where rules are first-class objects you can hot-swap, fork, and watch fight."
01SIGNALTagline is excellent. Promote it from page title to permanent masthead.
02RISKHyperdraft reads as four cloned TCG UIs. The interesting thing — the shared event pipeline — is invisible to anyone who isn't reading the README.
03VOICE"Theorycrafting / AI playtesting / novel mechanic exploration" is the right phrasing. Lean further into lab, away from game site.
04RESTRAINTAvoid mimicking any of the eight source games' chromes. Originality on the wrapper is what unifies them.
02Information architecture

Eight engines is a feature, but right now it's organized like an accident.

The product has at least four orthogonal axes — engine (which of the eight rulesets), set (which card pool inside that engine), mode (deckbuild, play vs. AI, watch AI vs. AI, replay), and opponent tier (easy → medium → hard → ultra/LLM). The README handles this by talking about modes as if they're top-level products, but a new player navigating eight rulesets × four difficulties × N card sets needs a single home that makes the matrix legible.

The interceptor pipeline (TRANSFORM → PREVENT → RESOLVE → REACT) is the architectural core, and it is also the answer to the IA question. Engines are presets of the same machine. If the home page expresses that visually — same chassis, swappable engines, with the differences spelled out in metrics not metaphors — the eight-game proposition becomes obvious instead of overwhelming.

The custom engines and the SCP archetype viewer are the most underexploited surfaces. The README's most exciting paragraph is the one about ~4,400 fan-made cards with working interceptors. That's the moat. It deserves to be a destination, not a folder.

05PROPOSEA single canonical home: masthead, engine rack (eight items, ranked by completeness and AI tier), a "what's running right now" live ticker for AI-vs-AI matches.
06PROPOSETreat engine as a global selector, not a route — switching engine should feel like switching a lens, with the chassis (deckbuilder, board, replay) staying put.
07PROPOSEFirst-class "archetype viewer" surface — promote SCP / custom-set archetypes from a screen inside the deckbuilder to a top-level browse experience, like a card-museum.
08RISKDon't let the eight engines compete for visual hierarchy. Rank them. Two or three lead engines, the others as a rack.
03Game-board UX

The play surface needs a vocabulary, not four costumes.

Each of the four mainline TCGs has its own zones (lanes, fields, bench/active, monster/spell/trap), its own resolution model (priority + stack vs. chains vs. turn-locked vs. roundwise), and its own card anatomy. The temptation is to make Hyperdraft's board mimic each game's native UI. Resist it. Mimicry forfeits the whole point of the unified engine and forces you to compete on polish with the official clients — a fight you cannot win on a solo budget.

Better: design one engine-agnostic board grammar and let each ruleset configure it. Zones are labeled rows. Cards are abstract spec-sheets — name, cost, body stat, sub-stat, status — rendered identically across engines, so a Pokemon active and a Magic creature read with the same eyes. The cost system varies (mana / energy / crystals / tribute) but it's always the same chip in the same corner. Status (tapped, asleep, summoning-sick, awaiting-evolution) is one bar of glyphs.

The stack/chain is the most distinctive thing in the entire product and currently it's just there. Promote it. A right-side rail that shows which interceptor is firing and which is queued, named like a debugger, would make Hyperdraft feel like a piece of instrumentation and not a fan port.

Treat the board like an oscilloscope, not a tabletop. You're not pretending to be a card game; you're inspecting one.
09PROPOSEOne board template: opponent zones (top), shared stack/chain rail (right), phase rail (above hand), hand (bottom). Zone labels are slot-machine — they change per engine, layout doesn't.
10PROPOSECards are spec sheets, not painted faces. Use type, not illustration, to carry identity. Reserve a single corner for engine glyph + cost. This also sidesteps any IP question — never render branded card faces.
11PROPOSEMake the event log first-class. A scrollable, scrubbable feed that doubles as the replay timeline. Click a TRANSFORM event, see the state it produced. This is the demo-reel moment.
12RISKDrag-and-drop UX is fine, but combat-step priority will eat you if it's not visible. The phase rail must always be readable at a glance.

05Bold direction

If you push the identity, push it to "a debugger for the game of TCGs."

Most TCG sites cosplay as tabletops. They want the candle-lit pub feel. Hyperdraft cannot beat them at that, and shouldn't try. The pipeline is the asset. The interceptor model is the asset. The eight-engines-in-one-machine is the asset. The way to win is to refuse the tabletop frame entirely and build the visual identity of laboratory instrumentation — Bloomberg terminals, Are.na, a 1970s engineering reference card, the GLPK source-viewer, Recurse Center's old site.

Every card is a small spec sheet. Every match has a permalink and an event log. The "play" button is called "Open a match." The replay is the timeline; the timeline is the same widget on landing, in lobby, and on the board. The user can stop time, step the pipeline forward one event, and watch the interceptors light up — same way you'd step through a packet capture. That is a thing nobody else does. Lean all the way into it.

The aesthetic name I'd use for it internally is "polyglot card lab." Paper and ink. One sodium-orange accent. Tabular layouts. Cards as type, not portraiture. Stack and chain as bookkeeping, not pyrotechnics. The brand is the calm a developer feels when reading clean output. Eight engines, one frame, and the frame looks like the inside of the machine.

Don't compete with the tabletop. Build the part the tabletop can't show you: the rules themselves, running.
17BIG MOVEReplay is the timeline is the lobby. The same scrubber appears on landing showing "currently at turn 4 of HD-8F4A," on the board, and on the post-match page. One widget, three jobs.
18BIG MOVE"Pipeline view" toggle on the board (⌥P): replaces the cards with their event stream. TRANSFORM/PREVENT/RESOLVE/REACT columns, events as lines. This is the demo you put in the README hero.
19BIG MOVEPublic match URLs. hyperdraft.com/m/HD-8F4A. Anyone can watch any AI match. This is your distribution.
20BIG MOVERules diff. Two engines side-by-side: "MTG vs. Hearthstone for the same TURN_START event." Inspectable. This is what makes the multi-engine premise sing.

Pick the lab, not the playground — and everything else lines up.

Right now Hyperdraft has the bones of a great research tool and the surface of an unfinished fan port. The fix isn't more polish on the existing surface; it's a different posture. Lab posture. Tabular, monochrome, instrumented, calm. The eight engines stop being a burden and become the proof.
